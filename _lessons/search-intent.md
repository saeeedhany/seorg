## The four common intents

**Informational.** The searcher wants to learn something, as in "how to repot a snake plant" or "what is a canonical URL". Guides, explanations and how to articles fit this intent.

**Navigational.** The searcher wants a specific site or page, as in "gmail login" or "ikea careers". Only the destination itself will rank, so there is usually little to gain unless it is your own brand.

**Commercial.** The searcher is researching before a decision, as in "best running shoes for flat feet" or "notion vs obsidian". Comparisons, reviews and buying guides fit this intent.

**Transactional.** The searcher is ready to act, as in "buy trail running shoes size 9" or "plumber near me". Product pages, category pages, booking pages and local listings fit this intent.

Some queries have **mixed intent**. "Coffee grinder" might mean "show me grinders to buy" or "which kind should I get". Google then shows a mix of page types, and you can see which kind dominates.

## The results page is the brief

Labels are a starting point. The real guide is the results page itself, because it shows what Google has learned satisfies people. Before you write, search the query and study the top results.

**Format.** Is it guides, lists, product pages, videos, a calculator or forum threads? If every result is a list of products, a long essay will struggle.

**Depth.** Are the results quick answers or thorough guides? Match the depth searchers clearly want. That is different from matching a word count.

**Angle.** Do the top results share an angle, such as "for beginners", "in 2025" or "on a budget"? A shared angle shows what searchers value.

**Freshness.** Are the results recent? If so, the topic changes and your page will need regular updates.

**Search features.** A featured snippet, People also ask, videos or a local pack show which other needs the query carries.

**Gaps.** What do the top pages miss? Read People also ask and community threads on the topic. A question that keeps coming up but is badly answered is your opportunity to be more useful.

## Small words change everything

The same core topic has very different intents depending on a word or two:

1. **"project management software"** is broad research. Searchers want to understand the options.
2. **"best project management software"** means building a shortlist. Searchers want ranked comparisons.
3. **"project management software for agencies"** is a specific need. Searchers want options suited to agencies.
4. **"monday vs asana"** is the final comparison. Searchers want a direct, two way comparison.
5. **"asana pricing"** is close to buying. The pricing page is what searchers want.

Each of these needs a different page.

## Micro intent

Within one intent there is often a more specific question. For "how to fix a slow website" the searcher might want to *diagnose* the cause, *prioritize* what to fix first, or *apply* one particular fix. The best pages are clear about which of these they serve, and they make it easy to jump to the right section.

## A practical workflow

1. Write down the query exactly as a searcher would type it.
2. Search it in a private window and study the top five results: format, depth, angle and freshness.
3. Read People also ask and a community thread or two for the questions people actually have.
4. Write one sentence that states what your page will do for this searcher. For example: "This page helps a first time owner repot a snake plant safely, with the soil, pot size and timing they need."
5. Build the outline from that sentence, not from a list of keywords.

> **Common mistake.** Writing the page you want to publish rather than the page the searcher wants. A sales page will not rank for a "how to" query, however well it is optimized.
