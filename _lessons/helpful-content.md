## People first content

Google's guidance on helpful content asks one central question: was this page made mainly to help people, or mainly to attract search traffic? Its systems try to reward the first and demote the second. Since March 2024 this assessment is part of Google's core ranking systems, not a separate update.

Google publishes self assessment questions. These are the ones that matter most in practice:

1. Does the content provide **original information**, reporting, research or analysis?
2. Does it give a **substantial, complete** description of the topic?
3. Does it go **beyond the obvious**, adding insight rather than repeating other sources?
4. If it draws on other sources, does it **add real value** instead of just rewriting them?
5. Would a reader **leave satisfied**, without needing to search again?
6. Is it written by someone who **clearly knows the topic**?
7. Is it **free of easily checked factual errors**?

## Signs of search engine first content

Google also lists warning signs. A site showing several of them is likely to struggle:

1. Producing lots of content on many unrelated topics in the hope that some of it ranks
2. Using extensive automation to produce content on many topics
3. Mainly summarizing what others say without adding value
4. Writing about trending topics only because they are trending, not because your audience needs them
5. Writing to a particular word count because you heard Google prefers it
6. Entering a niche with no real expertise, mainly for search traffic
7. Promising to answer a question that has no answer, such as a release date that has not been announced
8. Changing dates on pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed

## Showing experience and expertise

The [ranking lesson]({{ '/learn/ranking-signals/' | relative_url }}) introduced E-E-A-T. Here is how it shows up on a real page.

**Experience shows in the details.** Original photos, test results, measurements, mistakes you made and what you learned. A hotel review that mentions the noisy lift on floor three was written by someone who stayed there.

**Expertise shows in accuracy and depth.** Correct terms, careful explanations of trade offs, and knowing which details matter. For YMYL topics such as health and finance, formal qualifications matter more.

**Authority comes from others.** It shows when other respected sites cite you, link to you or mention you. You earn it over time and cannot declare it.

**Trust is the foundation.** That means accurate content, sources for factual claims, clear ownership, contact details, honest disclosure of affiliate links and sponsorships, and secure pages.

## Who, how and why

Google suggests that every page should make three things clear.

**Who** made it. Add a byline where readers would expect one, linked to an author page that explains the person's background.

**How** it was made. For reviews, explain how you tested. For research, explain the method. If automation or AI tools played a large part, consider saying so where readers would reasonably want to know.

**Why** it was made. The answer should be "to help readers". If the honest answer is "to rank for this keyword", the page will probably not do well.

## AI and automation

Google's guidance says it rewards helpful content however it is produced. Using AI tools is not against its rules. What is against its rules is **scaled content abuse**: generating many pages mainly to manipulate rankings, without adding value for readers, whether by AI, templates, scraping or people.

In practice, if you use AI tools:

1. A knowledgeable person should check every fact, because generated text can state false things confidently.
2. Add what only you have: experience, data, examples and judgment.
3. Do not publish pages faster than you can make them genuinely good.

> **Common mistake.** Treating an author bio as an "E-E-A-T fix". A bio helps readers judge credibility, but it cannot make thin or inaccurate content trustworthy. The content itself has to show the expertise.
